# VLMD Subtask 1 Annotation Guidelines

## 1. Task Overview

For each row in the spreadsheet, you will see one claim and its immediate children. A claim and how its children work to prove the claim is an argument. Your job is to identify the psychological manipulation, which are called Cognitive Mechanisms, used to move from those children to that claim, if any manipulation is being done at all.

| Annotation Question | What to enter |
|---|---|
| Mechanism(s) | Mechanisms present, or NONE |
| Mechanism Justification(s) | Short reason tied to how each mechanism is achieved in the argument |

## 2. Use Only the Immediate Children

Classify each argument independently. Ask: "How are ONLY the provided children being used to make the reader accept this claim?"

- There are multiple arguments in a single meme that you will annotate, so do not automatically copy/bubble up a mechanism from an earlier argument.
- Do not judge the meme as a whole. Judge the use of the children to prove the claim in the current argument.
- If the child as established directly supports the claim and no mechanism is needed, even if that child was established with a mechanism previously in the meme, choose NONE.

## 3. The Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Affective Hijacking (AH) | A visceral emotional reaction is mapped onto the claim, substituting feeling for evidence. | A close-up photo of an oversized syringe piercing a crying child's skin → the claim that vaccines are a form of medical abuse. |
| Episodic Overgeneralization (EO) | A specific example, event, behavior, or image is stretched into a broader trait, pattern, or group-level conclusion. | A single photo of a snowstorm in one city → the claim that global warming is a hoax. |
| Spurious Causal Correlation (SCC) | Two unrelated elements are placed in visual proximity to force an unearned cause-and-effect relationship. | A photo of a 5G tower positioned beside a photo of dead birds → the claim that cellular radiation is killing wildlife. |
| Satirical Trivialization (ST) | Mockery or exaggeration makes an idea look absurd, inviting dismissal without evaluation. | A serious climate-data chart with a cartoon of a hysterical, crying scientist superimposed over it → the claim that climate science is alarmist nonsense. |
| Esoteric Code Activation (ECA) | An outwardly innocuous symbol functions as a coded signal, legible only to an in-group audience. | A cartoon sheep wearing a medical mask → the claim, understood only within a specific subculture, that people who follow public health guidance are mindless conformists. |
| Spurious Legitimacy Signaling (SLS) | A person, group, or action is treated as credible, correct, or justified because it is endorsed by an authority, institution, the majority, or a socially accepted group rather than because of direct evidence. | A celebrity officially endorses a policy → The policy must be correct. |

## 4. Rules

| Mechanism | Rule | Advice | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Affective Hijacking | Emotion must match the facts. | Emotion that fits the facts is fine. Emotion that is bigger than the facts, or replaces missing facts, is AH. | Burned house + crying family → “Their house was destroyed.” (No AH) vs. small dent + “HE ALMOST DIED!!!” → “The crash was serious.” (AH) |
| Episodic Overgeneralization | Not every specific-to-general jump is EO. | Only EO if one case is stretched into a broad pattern without proof. | Missed all 3 meetings → “Missed every meeting this week.” (No EO) |
| ^ | EO vs. SCC | SCC: two unrelated things placed together to imply cause. EO: one real case stretched into a general rule. | One rude student → “All students are rude.” (EO) vs. energy drink + failed test → “Energy drinks cause failure.” (SCC) |
| Satirical Trivialization | Humor alone isn’t ST. | Only ST if mockery makes an idea look absurd instead of evaluating it. | Player holding trophy + funny caption → “He won the championship.” (No ST) |
| ^ | ST vs. AH | Humor or mockery points to ST. Add AH only if a separate emotion (fear, anger, sadness) is used. | Politician drawn as a clown → “His plan is a joke.” (ST) vs. same + “HE’LL RUIN YOUR FUTURE!!!” → “His plan is dangerous.” (ST + AH) |
| Spurious Legitimacy Signaling | An authority isn’t always SLS | Only SLS if the authority isn’t real evidence for the claim but is treated as proof. | Weather service warning → “A storm is coming.” (No SLS) |
| All mechanisms | Mechanisms in the elements still count even if the background proves the claim. | Annotate mechanisms in the elements even when the background already proves the claim. | Background: layoff news report. Element: crying child + “The CEO doesn’t care about your family” → “The company laid off 500 workers.” (AH) |
| ^ | Judge the concept, not the wording | Look past small word choices. Ask if the mechanism is conceptually present. | Team with trophy + “greatest team ever” → “The team won.” (No mechanism; ignore “greatest”) |
| ^ | Treat children as true | Judge how the children prove the claim, not whether they’re correct. | “Moon is cheese” → “Moon contains dairy.” (No Mechanism) |
| ^ | Rewrite meta-commentary claims | If the claim comments on the children, mentally rewrite it as a direct claim first. | “This analogy is absurd” → “The idea being compared is absurd.” |

## 5. Writing the Justification

Say what in the children is being used and what manipulative move it makes toward the argument. The justification should demonstrate how the named mechanism is being achieved in the argument.

- Good: "One image of several soldiers is used to establish what soldiers routinely endure."
- Good: "The exaggerated expression is used to make her appear frightening and therefore less trustworthy."
- Bad: "This feels manipulative."
- Bad: "This is affective hijacking."

## 6. Quick Checklist

1. Read the argument.
2. Focus only on its listed children.
3. Identify the reasoning moves from the children to the argument that match a mechanism.
4. List the mechanisms taking place to prove the claims.
5. Write a short justification on how each mechanism is being enacted in the argument.
