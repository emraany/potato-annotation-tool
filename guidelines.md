# VLMD Subtask 1 Annotation Guidelines

## 1. Task Overview

For each row in the spreadsheet, you will see one claim and its immediate children. A claim and how its children work to prove the claim is an argument. Your job is to identify the psychological manipulation, which are called Cognitive Mechanisms, used to move from those children to that claim, if any manipulation is being done at all.

| Annotation Question | What to enter |
|---|---|
| Does the Argument Pass the Sensible Test? | The result of the Sensible Test which is explained in-depth below. If ‘No’ is selected, there are no mechanisms present, and if ‘Yes’ is selected, there is at least one mechanism present |
| Mechanism(s) | Mechanisms present, or NONE |
| Mechanism Justification(s) | Short reason tied to how each mechanism is achieved in the argument |

## 2. The Sensible Test

The Sensible Test determines if the argument being made is driven by rational comprehension or rhetorical manipulation. Three questions are in this test:

1. **The Manipulation Check:** Is the creator using emotional hijacking, mockery, side-by-side tricks, out-of-context cropping, or coded dog-whistles to force this connection in lieu of evidence?
2. **The Deception Check:** Does a reader have to accept an unproven assumption, a warped timeline, or a structural lie for the child(ren) to successfully connect to the claim?
3. **The Rhetorical Wrapper Check:** Is the visual format being used as a psychological trap to lower the reader's critical thinking, rather than as a helpful tool to illustrate data or literal facts?

There are two cases:

- All of these questions can be answered with ‘No’ (the argument passes the Sensible Test) ⇒ fill the mechanisms with ‘None’
- One or more of these questions can be answered with ‘Yes ⇒ the argument must be examined for mechanisms as there is at least one mechanism present

## 3. Use Only the Immediate Children

Classify each argument independently. Ask: "How are ONLY the provided children being used to make the reader accept this claim?"

- There are multiple arguments in a single meme that you will annotate, so do not automatically copy/bubble up a mechanism from an earlier argument.
- Do not judge the meme as a whole. Judge the use of the children to prove the claim in the current argument.
- If the child as established directly supports the claim and no mechanism is needed, even if that child was established with a mechanism previously in the meme, choose NONE.

## 4. The Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Affective Hijacking | A visceral emotional reaction is mapped onto the claim, substituting feeling for evidence. | A close-up photo of an oversized syringe piercing a crying child's skin → the claim that vaccines are a form of medical abuse. |
| Episodic Overgeneralization | A specific example, event, behavior, or image is stretched into a broader trait, pattern, or group-level conclusion. | A single photo of a snowstorm in one city → the claim that global warming is a hoax. |
| Spurious Causal Correlation | Two unrelated elements are placed in visual proximity to force an unearned cause-and-effect relationship. | A photo of a 5G tower positioned beside a photo of dead birds → the claim that cellular radiation is killing wildlife. |
| Satirical Trivialization | Mockery or exaggeration makes an idea look absurd, inviting dismissal without evaluation. | A serious climate-data chart with a cartoon of a hysterical, crying scientist superimposed over it → the claim that climate science is alarmist nonsense. |
| Esoteric Code Activation | An outwardly innocuous symbol functions as a coded signal, legible only to an in-group audience. | A cartoon sheep wearing a medical mask → the claim, understood only within a specific subculture, that people who follow public health guidance are mindless conformists. |
| Spurious Legitimacy Signaling | A person, group, or action is treated as credible, correct, or justified because it is endorsed by an authority, institution, the majority, or a socially accepted group rather than because of direct evidence. | A celebrity officially endorses a policy → The policy must be correct. |

## 5. Rules

| Rule | Advice | |
|---|---|---|
| Humor does not always mean Satirical Trivialization | Satirical Trivialization is only present when mockery or humor makes an idea look absurd instead of evaluating it. If the children still prove the claim alongside the humor, it passes the Sensible Test. | Player holding a championship trophy + funny caption → “The player won the championship.” |
| The use of emotion does not always mean Affective Hijacking | Emotion is only a mechanism when it's used improperly, substituting feeling for evidence. Emotion that fits and supports the claim is acceptable. | Photo of a burned-down house with the family crying → “Their house was destroyed.” |
| Limited evidence does not directly indicate a failing Sensible Test | Memes are short by nature, so judge whether the jump from children to claim is rational. Intuitive claims generally need less evidence. | “Closed Today” sign as the only child → “The store is closed.” |
| Not every specific-to-general link is Episodic Overgeneralization | It must move from specific to general, stretching one case into a broad trait or pattern. It is not Episodic Generalization if the jump is logical and proven. | Missed all 3 meetings → “Missed every meeting this week.” |
| The difference between Episodic Generalization and Spurious Causal Correlation | Spurious places two unrelated things together to imply cause and effect. Episodic starts from a real connection but stretches a single case into a general conclusion. | One rude student → “All students are rude” vs. energy drink + failed test → “Energy drinks cause failure.” |
| Mentioning an authority is not always Spurious Legitimacy Signaling | The authority must not be sufficient evidence to prove the claim, yet is being treated as it is. If the authority can act as meaningful evidence, this mechanism is not present | Weather service warning from a government agency → “A storm is coming.” |
| Mechanisms do not carry over from earlier in the meme | Judge each argument on its own. If the child, as established, directly supports the claim, choose NONE. | Earlier claim was manipulated. That claim becomes a current child and proves claim → NONE. |
| Arguments are not insensible just because a child is illogical/incorrect | Children should be treated as truth. The Sensible Test and the mechanisms should be derived from how the children prove the argument, not from the children themselves. | “Moon is cheese” → “Moon contains dairy.” |
| Action to take towards meta-commentary claims | If the claim is making a meta-commentary on the children, then the claim must be converted to a non-meta-commentary version before looking for mechanisms | “This analogy is satirical and absurd” → The idea being compared is absurd.” |

## 6. Writing the Justification

Say what in the children is being used and what manipulative move it makes toward the argument. The justification should demonstrate how the named mechanism is being achieved in the argument.

- Good: "One image of several soldiers is used to establish what soldiers routinely endure."
- Good: "The exaggerated expression is used to make her appear frightening and therefore less trustworthy."
- Bad: "This feels manipulative."
- Bad: "This is affective hijacking."

## 7. Quick Checklist

1. Read the argument.
2. Focus only on its listed children.
3. Complete the Sensible Test for this argument by answering the three checks.
4. If the answer to all 3 checks were ‘No’, there are no mechanisms and those boxes can be filled with ‘None’. If any one of the checks are answered with ‘Yes’, the row must be filled in with the mechanism(s).
5. Identify the reasoning move from the children to the argument based on the failed check(s) in the Sensible Test.
6. List the mechanisms taking place to prove the claims.
9. Write a short justification on how each mechanism is being enacted in the argument.
